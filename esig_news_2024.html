Ethiopian Sugar Industry Group Posts Historic ETB 15.6 Billion Revenue in the Year Ending June 2024, Nearly Doubling the Previous Year as Domestic Production Climbs to 163,290 Tonnes

The state enterprise recorded its highest annual revenue since inception — yet a confirmed net loss of ETB 9.6 billion persists, underscoring that operational recovery and financial self-sufficiency remain two very different destinations.

By Tigist Woldesenbet, Senior Business Correspondent 22 January 2025 Addis Ababa

The Ethiopian Sugar Industry Group (ESIG) disclosed on Tuesday that total revenue for the fiscal year ended 30 June 2024 reached ETB 15.6 billion — a 91.9 percent increase over the previous year and the highest figure in the group's history. The results, confirmed through official ESIG communications and corroborated by The Reporter Ethiopia, mark what analysts are describing as a structural inflection point for the state-owned enterprise — even as the broader Ethiopian economy was simultaneously undergoing its most dramatic macroeconomic restructuring in fifty years.

The revenue surge was driven almost entirely by a dramatic expansion in domestic sugar production. ESIG's factories — including the newly commissioned Omo Kuraz II and Omo Kuraz III facilities in the South Omo Zone — collectively produced 163,290 metric tonnes of sugar in the 12 months ending 30 June 2024, a 34.8 percent increase over the estimated 121,500 tonnes recorded the year before. Sales volumes reached 166,603 tonnes, slightly exceeding production as the group drew on existing inventory to meet robust domestic demand. For ESIG, the timing of this operational breakthrough was both fortuitous and complicated: it coincided precisely with the year Ethiopia's macroeconomic environment was shaken by a devaluation, an IMF bailout, and a currency shock that sent ripples through every company carrying foreign-currency denominated debt — a category that describes ESIG almost perfectly.

Ethiopia in 2024 — The Year the Birr Collapsed

On 29 July 2024, the National Bank of Ethiopia abandoned its fifty-year-old fixed exchange rate regime as a core condition for receiving a USD 3.4 billion IMF Extended Credit Facility. The Birr, which had traded at 57.48 to the US dollar on 26 July, fell to 74.73 Birr per dollar within days and continued depreciating to over 103 Birr per dollar by mid-August 2024. In less than three weeks, the currency had lost roughly 80 percent of its value. The government, led by Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed, responded with an ETB 550 billion supplementary budget, 40 percent of which was earmarked for subsidies on critical imports including fuel and medicine. On 1 August 2024, the Addis Ababa City Trade Bureau ordered the temporary shutdown of 71 businesses caught making "unreasonable price increases" on imported goods purchased at the pre-devaluation rate. Inflation, already running at approximately 26.6 percent during the 2023/24 period, accelerated further — though Ethiopia's economy nonetheless expanded at 7.3 percent over that same period, driven by industry at 9.2 percent and agriculture at 7.0 percent.

The IMF reforms — which also included removing fuel subsidies, partially privatising state enterprises, and permitting foreign banks to operate for the first time in Ethiopia's modern history — were framed by Prime Minister Abiy's government as a necessary reckoning to cure the country's chronic foreign exchange shortage, which had seen official reserves fall below one billion US dollars and the parallel market rate diverge from the official rate by more than one hundred percent. For ESIG, which carries tens of billions of Birr in foreign-currency debt, the overnight devaluation had an immediate and painful arithmetic consequence: the Birr value of its outstanding loans, and the interest payments on them, surged proportionally. This is a dynamic that will colour the group's next set of accounts even more acutely than the year ending June 2024.

The Production Story: Omo Kuraz Changes the Equation

Set against this turbulent macroeconomic backdrop, ESIG's production results stand as a genuinely positive achievement. The 163,290 tonnes produced in the 12 months ending June 2024 represent the culmination of over a decade of capital-intensive investment in Ethiopia's Omo Valley — investments that generated enormous debt but sat largely unproductive while factories remained under construction or operating below capacity.

Sugar production over five years (metric tonnes): Year ending 2020: ~90,000 t; year ending 2021: ~95,000 t; year ending 2022: ~100,000 t; year ending 2023: ~121,500 t; year ending June 2024 (confirmed): 163,290 t (▲ 34.8%). Source: ESIG Official Portal (etsugar.com) — directly reported figure.

Omo Kuraz II and III — two of the most expensive infrastructure projects in Ethiopian corporate history — have finally begun generating the volumes their price tags demanded. The group also recorded a modest but symbolically significant cost saving of ETB 10.8 million by performing land-levelling work at Omo Kuraz through its own workforce rather than outsourcing to external contractors, signalling improving internal operational capability. Molasses revenue, a by-product of the refining process, is expected to approach ETB 900 million in the year ending June 2024 as output scales proportionally with sugar production, further diversifying revenue beyond the group's historical sugar-and-import dependence.

At the beginning of the five-year window, imported sugar — purchased internationally and resold on the domestic market — constituted 50.6 percent of ESIG's total revenue. It was a high-cost, low-margin activity that exposed the group to global commodity price volatility. By the year ending June 2024, that share had fallen to an estimated 8 percent, with domestically produced sugar now dominant. The result is a gross profit margin estimated at 53.8 percent in the year ending June 2024 — up from just 13.7 percent at the start of the period and the best reading in the five-year comparison.

"For the first time in years, domestic production is carrying the revenue story. The dependence on expensive imported sugar that crippled our margins is being decisively reversed."
— Senior ESIG official, speaking to this newspaper on condition of anonymity

The Loss That Remains: Structural, Not Operational

Despite the revenue breakthrough, ESIG recorded a confirmed net comprehensive loss of approximately ETB 9.6 billion in the year ending June 2024 — the second-lowest annual loss in the five-year window after the previous year’s ETB 9.57 billion. The persistent loss reflects structural financial burdens that no operational improvement alone can resolve in the near term.

Revenue vs. net loss across the period (ETB billions): Year ending 2020 revenue: ETB 9.93B; year ending 2020 net loss: (ETB 10.98B). Year ending 2022 revenue: ETB 8.57B; year ending 2022 net loss (worst year): (ETB 19.13B). Year ending June 2024 revenue (confirmed): ETB 15.6B; year ending June 2024 net loss (confirmed): (ETB 9.6B).

Foreign exchange losses — the single largest destroyer of value in ESIG's income statement across the study period — amounted to an estimated ETB 3.6 billion in the year ending June 2024, confirmed by official sources. While substantially reduced from the catastrophic ETB 10.5 billion peak earlier in the period, this figure will almost certainly increase in the coming years as the full impact of the July 2024 Birr devaluation flows through to the Birr valuation of ESIG's remaining foreign-currency loans. Every significant fall in the Birr mechanically adds billions to those outstanding balances.

Finance costs — interest on the remaining ETB 50 to 70 billion in loans not yet transferred to the Land Administration and Management Corporation (LAMC) or the Ministry of Finance — are estimated at approximately ETB 5.0 billion for the year ending June 2024. Together, forex losses and interest charges are estimated to have consumed approximately ETB 8.6 billion, narrowly exceeding the group's estimated gross profit of ETB 8.4 billion.

Ethiopia in 2024 — Agriculture, BRICS and the Economy

January 2024 saw Ethiopia formally join the BRICS economic alliance alongside Egypt, Iran, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE — a diplomatic milestone Prime Minister Abiy's government presented as evidence of Ethiopia's growing stature in the Global South. On the agricultural front, the government's support programme — which included ETB 126.8 billion in agricultural input subsidies and ETB 16 billion in fertilizer assistance — contributed to a record Meher harvest of 506.8 million quintals, up 61 percent from the prior year. Horticulture production grew from 122.6 million to 340.3 million quintals in 2024. The agricultural sector's 7 percent growth provided a broadly positive backdrop for ESIG's own production expansion, even as security tensions in the Amhara region — where a state of emergency ran from August 2023 through June 2024 — continued to constrain activity in parts of the country.

Balance Sheet: Nominal Stability Built on Sovereign Foundations

ESIG's balance sheet at the close of the year ending June 2024 reflects nominal stability as a direct consequence of the government's extraordinary debt restructuring over the two years before. Through that exercise, approximately ETB 180 billion in ESIG's loans were transferred to LAMC and the Ministry of Finance — one of the largest sovereign financial interventions in Ethiopian corporate history. Total liabilities are estimated at approximately ETB 77 billion, down from a peak of ETB 242.9 billion in 2021. Total equity stands at approximately ETB 99.4 billion, reversing the deeply negative equity position of ETB 45.4 billion recorded just three years earlier.

The cash position, however, tells a more sobering story. Cash and cash equivalents stood at only ETB 1.5 billion — confirmed by official sources — against current liabilities estimated at ETB 18 billion. The resulting cash ratio of 0.083 times means ESIG can cover less than nine percent of its immediate obligations from available funds. Despite the 91.9 percent revenue surge, meaningful cash generation has not materialised after finance costs are settled, confirming the group's continued dependence on government financial disbursements for operational liquidity.

Efficiency: The First Meaningful Reversal in Four Years

Analysts point to efficiency metrics as the clearest evidence of structural change in the year ending June 2024. Total asset turnover — how much revenue the group generates per Birr of assets held — is estimated to have nearly doubled to 0.088 times from 0.042 times a year earlier, ending four consecutive years of decline. Fixed asset turnover crossed 0.1 times for the first time in the study period. The interest coverage ratio improved to an estimated minus 0.70 times — the closest the group has come to being able to service its debt from operations — from minus 3.42 times at its weakest point in the period.

Efficiency & solvency snapshot (previous year vs year ending June 2024): Total asset turnover rose from 0.042x to 0.088x (estimate). Inventory turnover improved from 0.67x to 0.99x (estimate). Interest coverage improved from –3.42x (worst reading in the period) to –0.70x (estimate), bringing the ratio closer to break-even.

A value of minus 0.70 times on interest coverage means ESIG would need to add approximately ETB 8.5 billion in additional operating income to cover its interest obligations at a ratio of 1.0 times. At current revenue growth momentum, this milestone appears calculable rather than theoretical for the first time since the five-year analysis began.

The Road to Profitability — A Number, At Last

Analysts contacted by this newspaper estimate that ESIG would require revenues in the range of ETB 25 to 30 billion at current cost levels to achieve net profitability. That figure — which would have seemed entirely out of reach two years ago when revenue stood at ETB 7.42 billion — is now within the range of possibility within two to three reporting cycles if revenue growth of thirty to forty percent annually continues. The gross profit margin at an estimated 53.8 percent means the operational machinery is finally generating meaningful surplus above production costs.

The central constraint, as it has been throughout the study period, is not operational management. It is the structural legacy of decisions made before any current ESIG executive took office. The government borrowed at international rates, in foreign currencies, to build factories that took far longer than planned to commission. The resulting debt mountain — even after ETB 180 billion was transferred to LAMC and the Ministry of Finance — continues to generate ETB 5 billion or more per year in interest charges. And the July 2024 Birr devaluation, while necessary for Ethiopia's macroeconomic stability, has mechanically increased the Birr value of the remaining foreign-currency obligations.

Whether that arithmetic improves or worsens in the years ahead depends on three factors: the trajectory of the Birr, the pace of continued debt restructuring by LAMC and the Ministry of Finance, and the commissioning speed of the remaining factory capacity — particularly Omo Kuraz I and V, which remain incomplete. The full audited financial statements for the year ending June 2024 have not been published. ESIG has historically experienced audit delays of two to three years; the audit covering the year ending June 2022 was only signed in September 2024.

Ethiopia 2024 — Key Developments

The Birr Devalues 80% in 10 Days

On 29 July 2024, Ethiopia abandoned its fixed exchange rate regime as a condition of a USD 3.4 billion IMF loan. The Birr fell from 57.48 to over 103 per dollar by mid-August — one of the fastest depreciations on the continent in recent memory. Fuel prices nearly doubled in Birr terms.

Ethiopia Joins BRICS

In January 2024, Ethiopia formally became a BRICS member alongside Egypt, Iran, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE, expanding the bloc to 10 nations.

Record Meher Harvest

Government agricultural subsidies totalling ETB 142.8 billion contributed to a Meher harvest of 506.8 million quintals — up 61% on the prior year. Horticulture output nearly tripled.

Economy Grows 7.3%

Despite the currency shock and 26.6% inflation, Ethiopia's economy expanded 7.3% in the 2023/24 period, led by industry at 9.2%.

Amhara Emergency Lifted

A state of emergency declared in Amhara in August 2023 following Fano militia clashes was lifted in June 2024, though security conditions remained fragile in parts of the region.

GERD Inauguration Planned

Prime Minister Abiy confirmed plans to formally inaugurate the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam following the end of the rainy season, with invitations extended to Egypt, Sudan, and all Nile Basin countries.

Ethiopia 2024
Major Economic Developments in Ethiopia in 2024 (With Sources)

In 2024, Ethiopia’s economy combined strong headline growth with major macroeconomic adjustment. Real output expansion remained among the fastest in Africa, while the government and the National Bank of Ethiopia advanced a reform package that centered on exchange‑rate liberalisation, tighter monetary policy, and an IMF‑supported financing framework. The result was a year defined by both momentum and stress: growth continued, but inflation, foreign‑exchange constraints, and debt‑service pressure shaped day‑to‑day economic conditions for households and firms.

1) Growth: a strong year, with multiple estimates converging near 8%

Several publicly available datasets point to Ethiopia recording roughly an eight‑percent pace of real GDP growth in 2024, reflecting continued recovery from conflict‑related shocks and the contribution of public investment and services activity. Estimates differ slightly by source and methodology, but they cluster within a narrow band.

CountryEconomy’s compilation of national accounts data reports real GDP growth of about 8.1% in 2024 (up from about 7.2% in 2023), while other international databases show similar magnitudes around the mid‑to‑high 7% range. Trading Economics’ quarterly series also indicates a strong 2024 growth profile consistent with a near‑8% annual pace.

Source: CountryEconomy — Ethiopia GDP  |  Source: Trading Economics — Ethiopia GDP Growth

2) Sectoral performance: broad‑based expansion

UNDP‑aligned economic profiles describe Ethiopia’s 2024 growth as broad‑based across agriculture, industry, and services. Agriculture continued to underpin employment and food supply, industry benefited from construction and manufacturing activity, and services growth was supported by transport, trade, and telecom‑related activity. These sectoral dynamics matter because they shape not only GDP, but also foreign‑exchange demand (imports for industry and infrastructure) and inflation sensitivity (food prices and logistics costs).

Source: UNDP — Ethiopia Quarterly Economic Profile (April 2025, includes 2024 context)

3) IMF program: Extended Credit Facility and the reform anchor

Ethiopia’s reform agenda in 2024 was closely linked to external financing and policy credibility. The IMF approved a multi‑year Extended Credit Facility (ECF) arrangement, and subsequent IMF communications emphasized progress on the government’s “homegrown” reform program, including steps toward a market‑determined exchange rate and broader macro stabilisation. The ECF framework also created a platform for donor alignment and for continued monitoring through reviews.

Source: IMF — Staff‑Level Agreement / ECF Review (Nov 2024)

4) Exchange‑rate liberalisation: the July 2024 shift

A defining turning point was the National Bank of Ethiopia’s July 2024 move toward a more market‑based foreign‑exchange system. Reporting from international outlets described a large one‑day depreciation at the start of the new regime. IMF communications later noted that foreign‑exchange market conditions improved, with reduced spreads between official and parallel rates compared with earlier periods of acute FX shortage. In practical terms, the adjustment aimed to improve FX allocation and reduce distortions, while raising short‑term inflation and balance‑sheet risks for borrowers with foreign‑currency exposure.

Source: Reuters — Ethiopia shifts to market‑based FX system (Jul 2024)  |  Source: IMF — FX market observations in ECF review

5) Inflation and monetary policy: tightening amid high prices

Inflation remained a central policy challenge. UNDP economic profiles discuss a period of elevated inflation and the importance of monetary restraint, including steps aimed at reducing monetary financing and bringing money‑supply growth down from earlier peaks. As inflation conditions evolved, the policy narrative focused on balancing macro stabilisation with the social impact of price pressures, particularly on urban cost of living.

Source: UNDP — Ethiopia Quarterly Economic Profile (Jan 2024)

6) Debt and external pressure: restructuring talks and repayment risks

Ethiopia entered 2024 with heavy external‑debt pressure and ongoing restructuring discussions with official creditors under the G20 Common Framework context. World Bank materials and IMF‑related documentation emphasize that the reform program was also meant to support debt sustainability over time. Market attention during the year also focused on external repayment timelines, including Eurobond‑related concerns, because such obligations can constrain reserves and amplify FX‑market stress if not managed through restructuring or refinancing.

Source: World Bank document — Ethiopia macro/debt context

7) Trade, exports, and foreign‑exchange earnings: the constraint that shaped the year

Foreign‑exchange availability was the binding constraint behind many of Ethiopia’s macroeconomic decisions. The NBE’s own reporting highlights the tight reserve position around this period, reinforcing why FX reform, export mobilisation, and external financing were central policy priorities. Reporting in 2024/25 also highlighted improving export earnings, particularly from agriculture and minerals, which is crucial because stronger export receipts can help reduce import backlogs and narrow FX shortages over time.

Source: National Bank of Ethiopia — Annual Report 2023/24

8) Fiscal policy and reconstruction financing: rebuilding while consolidating

Public finance policy in 2024 reflected competing demands. On one side were reconstruction and humanitarian needs following major conflict and climate shocks. On the other was the need for fiscal discipline to support stabilisation and reduce inflationary financing. UNDP’s quarterly profiles discuss fiscal deficit trends and the broader effort to manage spending and revenue while maintaining priority outlays.

Source: UNDP — Fiscal context (Jan 2024 profile)

9) Investment climate and liberalisation signals: opening key sectors

Reform communications during and after 2024 highlighted intentions to deepen liberalisation and improve the investment climate, including opening elements of finance and services to greater competition and foreign participation. Investor sentiment, however, remained sensitive to currency volatility, inflation trends, and the credibility of the new FX regime. In practice, 2024 was a transition year: reforms advanced, but investors watched carefully for macro stability and predictable access to foreign exchange.

Source: Semafor — Ethiopia floats birr (context and investor reactions)

10) Humanitarian and social context: the cost of adjustment

Economic adjustment in 2024 occurred alongside large humanitarian needs. UNDP reporting highlights the scale of food insecurity and vulnerability, reinforcing why policymakers and donors emphasized social‑protection measures to cushion the impact of inflation and reform. For households, the main economic story was often not GDP growth, but the lived experience of prices, jobs, and access to essentials—especially in a year when currency adjustment reshaped the cost of imported goods and inputs.

Source: UNDP — Social/humanitarian context (Jan 2024 profile)

Ratio Analysis

Two Separate Stories, One Balance Sheet

The profitability picture ESIG presents for the year ending June 2024 is, in a sense, two entirely separate narratives compressed into a single set of accounts. At the gross margin level, there has been outstanding and consistent improvement — from 13.7 percent at the start of the five‑year window to an estimated 53.8 percent by the year ending June 2024 — driven by successful import substitution, expanding domestic production, and by-product revenue growth. This represents genuine operational achievement. At the net profitability level, losses persisted in every single year of the study period, ranging from ETB 9.6 billion to ETB 19.1 billion annually, driven by structural costs that operational management cannot unilaterally resolve.

As one Addis Ababa-based financial analyst described it to this newspaper: "ESIG is in the unusual position of being operationally improving and financially distressed simultaneously. A gross profit margin at 53 percent is genuinely world-class for a sugar manufacturer. But every Birr of that gross profit — and then some — is being funnelled into servicing debt that arose from decisions made in another era, at another exchange rate."

Key ratio highlights (estimates unless noted): Gross profit margin: 53.8% (best in five years, up from 13.7% at the start of the window). Net profit margin: –61.5% (recovering from –223.2% at the worst point in the period, but still negative as finance costs remain the main drag). Operating margin: –22.4% (strong improvement from the trough). Current ratio: 1.50x (above 1.0x, though asset quality is a concern). Cash ratio (confirmed): 0.083x (ETB 1.5B cash against ~ETB 18B current liabilities). Debt ratio: 43.8% (down from a technically insolvent position earlier in the period due largely to sovereign debt transfers). Return on assets: –5.42% (stable around –5.4% for two consecutive years).

The net margin recovery from minus 223.2 percent at the worst point in the period to an estimated minus 61.5 percent in the year ending June 2024 is meaningful. For the first time in the study period, a visible convergence toward zero is occurring. The improvement arises because revenue nearly doubled while net loss remained broadly similar to the previous year, and because forex losses stabilised at ETB 3.6 billion against a peak of roughly ETB 10.5 billion earlier in the period.

The timing of the July 2024 Birr devaluation cuts ambiguously for ESIG. On one hand, it mechanically increases the Birr value of remaining foreign-currency debt and could push future forex losses higher in the years ahead. On the other, it makes ESIG's domestic production significantly more competitive against any imported alternatives — reinforcing the structural margin improvement that has been the group's most consistent positive trend. Whether the net effect proves positive or negative for medium-term finances will depend heavily on the pace of debt restructuring and currency risk mitigation.

The Birr Devaluation's Dual Impact on ESIG

The July 2024 exchange rate liberalisation — the Birr losing roughly 80 percent of its value in under three weeks — has a complex and contradictory effect on ESIG specifically.

Negative: The Birr value of remaining foreign-currency debt (estimated at ETB 50–70 billion) rises with every further currency depreciation, mechanically inflating forex losses and finance costs in Birr terms in the years ahead.

Positive: Domestic sugar production becomes significantly more cost-competitive against imported alternatives, reinforcing the structural margin transformation. Any future export ambitions would also benefit considerably.

Impact on the year ending June 2024: Limited. The devaluation occurred on 29 July 2024 — effectively right after the reporting year closed — so its full force will be felt in the next accounting cycle.

Background
A Decade of Debt, a Year of Recovery

ESIG — restructured from the Ethiopian Sugar Corporation under Council of Ministers Regulation 500/2022 — is among Ethiopia's largest state-owned enterprises by asset value. It operates sugar estates and factories across the Rift Valley, Omo Valley, and Amhara regions, employing tens of thousands of Ethiopians directly and supporting hundreds of thousands in surrounding agricultural communities.

Across the early years in the five‑year window, the group recorded combined net losses exceeding ETB 47 billion, driven by forex losses that peaked at ETB 10.45 billion in a single year and finance costs that, at their worst, exceeded total revenue. The balance sheet turned technically insolvent for a period, with liabilities exceeding assets by up to ETB 45 billion.

The government's intervention — transferring approximately ETB 180 billion in ESIG loans to LAMC and the Ministry of Finance over the two years before the June 2024 reporting date — restored nominal solvency. But analysts consistently distinguish accounting restructuring from commercial recovery. The year ending June 2024 is the first in which ESIG's operational performance, not sovereign support, is primarily driving positive financial signals. Whether that momentum is sustained now depends on the Birr, the pace of debt restructuring, and the speed at which remaining incomplete factory capacity is commissioned.

Concerns
Three Red Flags Analysts Cannot Ignore
Red Flag 1 — The Cash Crisis

With only ETB 1.5 billion in confirmed cash against approximately ETB 18 billion in current liabilities, ESIG can cover less than nine percent of its immediate obligations from available funds. The revenue surge has not yet translated into meaningful cash accumulation after finance costs. Any interruption in government disbursements would expose a severe liquidity deficit within weeks.

Red Flag 2 — Interest Coverage Still Negative

For the fifth consecutive year, ESIG failed to generate sufficient operating income to cover its interest expense. Finance costs of approximately ETB 5 billion per year are structurally incompatible with current revenue levels. An interest coverage ratio of minus 0.70 times means the group still destroys cash value every year, even at record revenue levels.

Red Flag 3 — Persistent Audit Qualifications

Each audited year in the most recent four‑year run received a qualified audit opinion, flagging material issues in revenue recognition, inventory valuation, cost of sales, and property and equipment. Until these qualifications are resolved, the financial statements carry inherent uncertainty. The audit for the year ending June 2024 — expected in 2025–2026 given historical delays — will be a critical test.

Recommendations
What Analysts Say Must Happen
Immediate — ERP Cost Accounting

A unified ERP-based cost accounting system must be deployed across all operational factories within twelve months. The chronic absence of reliable cost data — flagged by auditors for four consecutive years — undermines pricing, capital allocation, and performance benchmarking. This single reform would resolve the most persistent audit qualification and give management reliable figures for the first time.

Short-Term — Currency Risk Mitigation

Following the July 2024 Birr devaluation, the urgency of negotiating re-denomination or hedging arrangements for the remaining ETB 50–70 billion in foreign-currency loans has intensified significantly. The Ministry of Finance must engage lenders including China Development Bank and ICBC to explore Birr re-denomination. Eliminating half the annual forex loss could move ESIG close to operating breakeven at current revenues.

Strategic — Resolve Stranded Projects

Welkait and Tendaho have carried going-concern uncertainties for four consecutive years without resolution. The Board and Ministry of Industry must decide within six months whether to privatise, form joint ventures, or conduct managed wind-downs. Continued indecision ties up an estimated ETB 18–30 billion in stranded assets and management bandwidth.

Transparency — Quarterly Reporting

ESIG should publish quarterly unaudited financial summaries. Audit delays of two to three years leave Parliament, creditors, and the public without timely information on a strategically vital enterprise. Even unaudited quarterly disclosures would substantially improve accountability and investor confidence.
